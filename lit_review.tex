\section{Positioning}

\subsection{Our paper's contributions}

\blue{partly filled out}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Documenting widening age gap
    \item Comprehensively testing lifecycle self-interest hypotheses
    \item Comparing explanatory power of circumstances, expectations, values, and beliefs in one survey
    \item A decomposition of zero-sum beliefs that accounts for attribution/blame in different contexts (in progress)
    \item Harmonizing online and long-running surveys to compare drivers of support for redistribution (and to decompose the age gap)
    \item Cross-country and within-US analysis to discover historical drivers of US belief shift/age gap (in progress)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Our survey's advantages}

\begin{itemize}
    \item We observe circumstances, expectations, values, and beliefs of an individual all in one survey, while others tend to focus on a specific mechanism or compare fairness and efficiency, meaning we're well positioned to compare relative importance of mechanisms (even if we reject some of our novel ones). 
    \item Our comprehensive treatment of the lifecycle economic self-interest hypothesis is unique. Detailed questions about one's current financial situation, expected income path, income uncertainty/risk, long-term AI/climate/housing/health risk, and time preference are rare or nonexistent in other academic surveys.
    \item Importance of zero-sum beliefs is relatively new and other papers do not decompose these views into specific targets/beneficiaries/programs like we do (CEOs, gender, diversity initiatives). Still, we could do a better decomposition in a second wave.
    \item Other unique/uncommon questions include: our visual tax policy proposal (more concrete than ``government should equalize incomes''), self-reported change in preferences plus why it changed (nonexistent elsewhere, could also be improved in second wave), incentive effects (less-common in large surveys), alternative policies to support low-income (useful for disentangling channels), and whether hard work and education has/will pay off (other measures of mobility more common).
    \item Our 2025 sample provides a valuable post-2024 view of beliefs and preferences, and we have a large sample in the 18-29 range (many surveys are noisier in that range).
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Econometric decompositions}

\citem{shorrocks2013decomposition}

Shapely decomposition: calculates each variable's marginal contribution across every possible ordering, then averages those contributions (the Shapley value).

\citem{huettner2012axiomatic}

Axiomatic arguments for decomposing goodness of fit according to Shapley and Owen values.

\citem{gelbach2016covariates}

Gelbach decomposition: uses the omitted-variable-bias identity to decompose the total coefficient change between a restricted and full regression.

\subsection{Survey design}

\citem{stantcheva2023run}

Guidance on the complete survey process, from the design of the questions and experiments to the recruitment of respondents and the collection of data to the analysis of survey responses.

\citem{haaland2023designing}

Survey of the emerging literature on information provision experiments. Outlines best-practices for how to measure beliefs; design the information intervention; measure belief updating; deal with confounds such as experimenter demand effects; and recruit respondents using online panels.

\citem{haaland2025understanding}

Surveying the recent literature using open-ended survey data to uncover mechanisms behind economic beliefs and behaviors. Describes different ways of eliciting open-ended responses and methods to analyze such data, focusing on LLMs.

\subsection{Question design}

\citem{strathman1994consideration}

Develops a measure of the extent to which people consider distant vs immediate consequences of potential behaviors.

\citem{dominitz1997using}

Designs questions to estimate a respondent's subjective probability distribution for next year's household income. 

\citem{armantier2017overview} 

An overview of the survey of consumer expectations, which includes near-term subjective financial expectations.

\citem{falk2018global}

An experimentally validated survey data set of time preference, risk preference, positive and negative reciprocity, altruism, and trust from 80,000 people in 76 countries.

\subsection{Age-period-cohort}

\citem{busemeyer2009attitudes}

Using the 1996 ISSP Role of Government for 14 OECD countries, one’s position in the life cycle is a more important predictor of welfare preferences than income, in particular for education spending. Some countries, such as the United States, show a higher salience of the age cleavage across all policy fields.

\citem{fullerton2010generational}

Explores APC effects in attitudes towards education, health, and Social Security spending using GSS 1984--2008. Does not conceive of public support and opposition as symmetric: important differences between supporting more spending, being satisfied with current spending, and opposing spending.

\citem{pittau2016attitude}

APC on GSS 1978-2010, finds that the old are more averse to redistribution than the past. A multilevel logistic model with time-varying slopes and two independent levels of variation helps capture temporal patterns net of age and cohort effects.

\citem{lin2018government}

HAPC model on Americans' support for income redistribution. Claims that while cohort effects exist, age effects are most important.

\citem{meuleman2019economic}

HAPC for Great Britain (BSAS) and USA (GSS). Finds that a cohort’s
redistributive preferences are related to the level of inequality experienced during its formative years.

\citem{ghitza2023great}

Builds a model of American presidential voting in which the cumulative impression left by political events determines the preferences of voters.

\citem{ogrady2023ideological}

Using estimates from 27 European countries 1981--2018, the young in Europe are relatively more libertarian: more socially liberal than the old in
most countries but also more opposed to taxation and government spending. Today’s differences over social issues and immigration are similar in size to the 1980s.

\citem{jocker2024growing}

APC on GSS and ANES finding that partisan sorting has increased across long-standing and emerging issues, while ideological divergence has not.





\section{Values and redistributive preferences}

\subsection{Culture}

\citem{alesina2011preferences}

Handbook review of preferences for redistribution. Emphasizes the role of historical experiences, cultural factors, and personal history.

\citem{luttmer2011culture}

Immigrants from high-preference countries are more likely to vote for pro-redistribution parties.

\subsection{Generosity/altruism}

\citem{roberts2006patterns}

A meta-analysis of how personality traits change across life cycle. During young adulthood (20--40), social dominance, conscientousness, and emotional stability increase. In old age, social vitality and openness decrease.

\citem{landier2022neoliberal}

Support for deviation from efficient solutions is strongly correlated with moral values as defined by Haidt (2013): care, fairness, loyalty and authority. While the traditional left-right divide spans some of this variation, an even bigger role is played by what they label ``individualism.''

\subsection{In-group preferences/universalism}

\citem{luttmer2001group}

Individuals decrease their support for welfare as the welfare recipiency
rate in their community rises and increase their support for welfare spending as the share of local recipients from their own racial group rises.

\citem{enke2020moral, enke2023moral}

Heterogeneity in adherence to universalist values (individual rights, justice, impartial fairness, and avoidance of externalities) versus communal/particularist values (community, loyalty, betrayal, respect, and tradition) helps explain modern U.S. voting shifts.



\citem{cutler2021older}

Older adults across the globe exhibit increased prosocial behavior but also greater in-group preferences.

\citem{londono2022impact}

Exposure to low-income peers caused high-income students to diversify their social networks, have more accurate perceptions of the income distribution, and support progressive redistribution.

\citem{alesina2023immigration}

Simply making respondents think about immigration before asking questions about redistribution makes them support less redistribution. The perception that immigrants are economically weaker and more likely to take advantage of the welfare system is strongly correlated with lower support for redistribution. 
% Information about the true shares and origins of immigrants does not change support for redistribution. 
% especially if immigrants are perceived as more likely to take advantage of the welfare system.
% narratives shape people’s views on immigration more deeply than hard facts.

\citem{fischbacher2023identity}

In a lab setting, ingroup favoritism is a strong motive for redistribution in general, and it is the most important motive for redistribution to increase or reverse inequality.

\citem{cappelen2025universalism}

In almost all countries, younger people and women are more universalist, and universalism is predictive of many left-wing political views.

\citem{paine2025attitudes}

Links moral values to views about debt forgiveness. Finds that the ``conservative''/communalist principal component of values correlates negatively with age and debt foregiveness.

\subsection{Social standing}

\citem{corneo2002individual}

%Conventional economic motivation is only one of the factors that drive individual preferences for governmental redistribution. 
In some countries, the desire to act in accordance with public values and the desire to obtain high social standing largely shape individual preferences for redistribution.

\citem{kuziemko2014last}

Low-income individuals might oppose redistribution because it could differentially help the group just beneath them.

\subsection{Fairness/deservingness/equal opportunity/egalitarianism}

\citem{fehr1999theory}

Introduces the concept of inequality aversion; equality per se having value.

\citem{fong2001social}

Self- and exogenous-determination beliefs have large and significant effects on support for
redistribution. A likely interpretation is that people are reciprocal or hold strong principles of equity.

\citem{alesina2005preferences}

People who believe that American society offers equal opportunities are more averse to redistribution.

\citem{cappelen2007pluralism}

In a lab setting, estimates the prevalence of three principles of distributive justice (strict egalitarianism, liberal egalitarianism, and libertarianism) and the distribution of the weight attached to fairness.

\citem{krawczyk2010glimpse}

In a lab experiment, average transfers were 20\% lower when winning was determined by performance in a task rather than by sheer luck. On the other hand, greater inequality of opportunity measured by dispersion of assigned probabilities of winning did not lead to higher transfers.

\citem{durante2014preferences}

Lab experiment to study how demand for redistribution of income depends on
self-interest, insurance motives, and social concerns relating to inequality and efficiency.

\citem{almaas2020cutthroat}

Americans and Norwegians differ significantly in fairness views, but not in the
importance assigned to efficiency. Causal evidence suggesting that fairness considerations are more fundamental for inequality acceptance than efficiency considerations.


\citem{stantcheva2021understanding}

Support for income and estate taxes is most strongly correlated with perceived fairness of inequality and taxation and broader views of the government, while efficiency concerns play a minor role.

\citem{alesina2021perceptions}

A policy decomposition shows that the perceived causes of racial inequities are the strongest predictors of support for race-targeted or redistribution policies.

\citem{cavaille2023fair}

Book claiming that people support policies which move the status quo closer to shared norms of fairness; whether an outcome is perceived as fair depends on how well it fits the norms of reciprocity (institutionalized solidarity) and proportionality (of rewards to effort).

\citem{bonnet2025explains}

Contrasts the main theories of preferences for redistribution in a unified empirical framework using nationally representative samples of Germany, Italy, Japan, Slovenia, the UK, and the US. Beliefs in fair opportunities for upward mobility are the strongest predictor of demand for redistribution, especially in the US, UK, and Germany.

\citem{almaas2026fairness}

An experiment with more than 65,000 individuals across 60 countries, shows that the source of inequality plays a substantially larger role for inequality acceptance than the efficiency cost of redistribution.

\citem{fehr2026social}

Uncovers three fundamentally distinct social preference types: predominantly selfish, inequality
averse, and altruistic individuals. Inequality averse and altruistic individuals display a much stronger support for redistribution, and for them, beliefs about the role of effort and luck for success are highly relevant.  Between the two, altruistic individuals are considerably less supportive of policies aimed at reducing the incomes of the rich.
% While inequality averse individuals display strong support for policies aimed at reducing the incomes of the rich, altruistic individuals are considerably less supportive of these policies.

\subsection{Policy framing}

\citem{cavaille2015two}

Redistribution conceived as ``taking from the rich'' and redistribution conceived as ``giving to the poor'' prime different individual motives: self-oriented income maximization on the one hand and other-oriented social affinity on the other.

\citem{kuziemko2023compensate}

Less-educated Americans differentially demand predistribution policies (e.g., a federal jobs guarantee, higher minimum wages, protectionism, and stronger unions), while more-educated Americans differentially favor redistribution (taxes and transfers).





\section{Beliefs and redistributive preferences}

\citem{stantcheva2026perceptions}

Economica--Coase lecture with a framework of self-interest vs. societal impact vs. mindsets.

\subsection{Zero-sum beliefs}

\citem{wilkins2015you}

When high status groups perceive discrimination against them, they espouse zero-sum beliefs.

\citem{davidai2019politics}

Conservatives view social inequalities---where the status quo is frequently challenged---as zero-sum, but liberals view economic inequalities---where the status quo has remained relatively unchallenged---as zero-sum.

\citem{ali2025political}

A strategic rationale for zero-sum thinking in elections. Asymmetric information and distributional considerations together make voters wary of policies supported by others.

\citem{chinoy2026zero}

A more zero-sum mindset is strongly associated with more support for government redistribution. Zero-sum thinking is associated with the degree of intergenerational upward mobility, immigration, and slavery of an individual and their ancestors.

\subsection{Perceptions about inequality/mobility}

\citem{kenworthy2007inequality}

Rising inequality doesn't necessarily increase perceived inequality; greater perceived inequality doesn't necessarily increase redistribution demand; and demand doesn't necessarily translate into policy.

\citem{norton2011building}

Individuals in the U.S. dramatically overestimate the degree of equality in the wealth distribution and select an even more equal distribution than their estimates.

\citem{kuziemko2015elastic} 

An information treatment has large effects on views about inequality but only slightly moves tax and transfer policy preferences. This is partially explained by respondents’ low trust in government.
% a disconnect between concerns about social issues and the public policies meant to address them

\citem{alesina2018intergenerational}

Pessimistic information about mobility and increases support for redistribution, but not for  right-wing respondents, possibly because they do not see the government as the solution.

\citem{stantcheva2021perceptions}

Literature review finding that it is not only (or even mainly) reality, but perceptions that shape support for policy. 

\subsection{Perceived relative position}

\citem{cruces2013biased}

Those who overestimate their relative position tend to demand higher levels of redistribution when informed of their true ranking.

\citem{karadja2017richer}

Individuals who are richer than they initially thought demand less redistribution.

\citem{hoy2021relatively}

Contrary to prevailing wisdom, people who are told they are relatively poorer than they thought are less concerned about inequality and are not more supportive of redistribution. 

\citem{hvidberg2023social}

People view inequalities within their co-workers and education groups as significantly more unfair than overall inequality, yet underestimate inequality the most exactly within these groups. Lower-ranked respondents in all groups tend to overestimate their own position among others.

\subsection{Trust in government}

\citem{peyton2020does}

Uses randomized experiments about political corruption to identify the effect of trust in government on support for redistribution. Estimated effects are substantively negligible, statistically indistinguishable from zero, and comparable to estimates from two placebo experiments.

\subsection{Societal equilibria}

\citem{piketty1995social}

Left-wing dynasties believing less in individual effort and voting for more
redistribution coexist with right-wing dynasties. 
% Persistent differences in perceptions about social mobility can generate persistent differences in redistribution across countries.

\citem{piketty1998self}

If a society infers better traits from one’s social status (implying they believe less in luck), then, ironically, income inequality may become more persistent. This is because the rich are more motivated to stay rich.

\citem{alesina2005fairness}

If a society believes that luck, birth, connections, and/or corruption determine wealth, it will levy high taxes, thus distorting allocations and making these beliefs self-sustained.

\citem{benabou2006belief}

Belief in a just world and redistributive politics. An “American” equilibrium with a high prevalence of just-world beliefs and a relatively laissez- faire public policy, and a “European” equilibrium with more pessimism and a more extensive welfare state.





\section{Economic self-interest and redistributive preferences}

\blue{From \cite{alesina2001doesn}: ``Economic models suggest that redistribution is a function of the variance and skewness of the pre-tax income distribution, the volatility of income, the social costs of taxation and the expected income mobility of the median voter.''}

\subsection{Income level}

\citem{meltzer1981rational}

Inequality increases demand for redistribution. An increase in mean income relative to the income of the decisive voter increases the size of government.

\citem{sears1990limited} 

Unless the short-term economic effect is very direct, self-interest is not a very significant or large predictor of policy preferences, and “symbolic” attitudes have much more influence.

\subsection{Veil of ignorance/ex-ante insurance}

\citem{harsanyi1953cardinal}

A risk-averse individual may prefer a more equal distribution because it protects against landing in a disadvantaged position.

\citem{rawls1971theory}

Theory of distributive justice. Veil of ignorance: individuals choose principles of justice without knowing their future position in society. Difference principle: social and economic inequalities must be arranged to benefit the least advantaged.

\citem{frohlich1987laboratory}

In a laboratory setting, tests Rawls' notion of distributive justice. The overwhelmingly preferred principle is not maximizing the welfare of the worst-off, but maximizing the average income with a floor constraint.

\subsection{Economic insecurity/income volatility}

\citem{varian1980redistributive}

Redistribution as insurance when earnings reflect luck. The optimal redistributive tax involves trading off the benefits due to social insurance with the costs due to reduced incentives.

\citem{eaton1980optimal}

Redistribution as insurance when earnings are uncertain.

\citem{sinn1995theory}

The welfare state can be seen as an insurance device that makes lifetime careers safer, increases risk taking and suffers from moral hazard effects. Constant returns to risk taking are likely to imply a redistribution paradox where more redistribution results in more inequality.

\citem{iversen2001asset}

Important dimensions of the welfare state are designed to make workers more willing to invest in firm- and industry-specific skills that increase their dependence on particular employers and their vulnerability to market fluctuations.

\citem{moene2001inequality}

A model combining redistributive self-interest and insurance motives. When benefits are directed toward employed individuals, the redistribution effect dominates. When benefits go to people without earnings, the insurance effect can dominate. Policy design matters.

\citem{cusack2006risks}

Using detailed risk-exposure measures from labor force surveys, finds great importance of labor force risks in shaping preferences for redistribution.

\citem{rehm2009risks}

Occupation-level rather than industry-level risk predicts redistributive preferences.

\citem{rehm2012insecure}

When the economically disadvantaged (low income) and economically insecure (high risk) represent two distinct groups, popular support for the welfare state is broader and opinion less polarized.

\citem{gartner2017individual}

In a representative sample of the Swedish population, documents an economically important relationship between risk aversion and the demand for redistribution.

\citem{thewissen2019automation}

Using survey data for 17 European countries 2002--2012, finds that vulnerability to automation is an important determinant of the demand for redistribution.

\citem{bossert2023economic}

Conditional on current economic resources, economic insecurity is associated with both
greater political participation and more support for conservative parties.

\citem{melcher2023economic}

Economic insecurity has a systematic and strong effect on the redistributive, class, and racial attitudes of Americans. Economic insecurity—one’s worry or anxiety regarding the potential of future economic hardship—and cognitive economic insecurity—an individual’s more or less dispassionate estimate of future economic hardship—often have distinct and complementary effects.

\subsection{Prospect of upward mobility}

\citem{ravallion2000wants}

Prospects of mobility in both directions influence demand for
redistribution: support for redistribution is higher amongst those who expect their welfare to fall, and this effect is strongest amongst the currently well-off.

\citem{benabou2001social}

Prospect of upward mobility (POUM) decreases demand for redistribution. However, the authors conclude the POUM channel is probably dominated by the demand for social insurance.

\citem{rainer2008subjective}

Using probabilistic expectations data from the German Socio-Economic Panel, establishes a link between self-reported expectations of
occupational mobility and preferences for redistribution.

\citem{cojocaru2014prospects}

Individuals' expectations of upward mobility reduce their preference for redistribution, but only when the degree of risk aversion is low. 

\subsection{Macro growth/efficiency}

\citem{piketty2014optimal}

A model where top earners respond to taxes through three channels: labor supply, tax avoidance, and compensation bargaining. Empirical evidence consistent with bargaining effects: top tax rate cuts are associated with top one percent pretax income shares increasing but not higher economic growth.

\subsection{Homeownership/wealth}

\citem{ansell2014political}

Argues that homeowners experiencing house price appreciation will become less supportive of redistribution and social insurance policies since increased house prices both increase individuals’ permanent income and the value of housing as self-supplied insurance against income loss.

\citem{steele2020wealth}

Financial wealth is more closely associated with attitudes about income equality, while home equity is more closely associated with attitudes about unemployment benefits. While the upper tier of financial wealth most strongly opposes income equality, the middle tier of home equity most strongly opposes unemployment benefits.

\citem{gale2020wealth}

Examines household wealth across birth cohorts and over time using data from the Survey of Consumer Finances.

\citem{furtado2025housing}

In Belgium, Germany, France, and Slovakia, homeownership polarizes young adults in terms of their welfare support. Housing unaffordability, however, coalesces young homeowners and renters by increasing redistribution support.

\subsection{Opportunities narrowing with age}

\citem{carstensen1999taking}

Socioemotional selectivity argues that people's perceived remaining time shapes the goals they prioritize. Younger perceive the future as relatively open-ended and older as relatively limited.

\subsection{Age-specific policy/power}

\citem{campbell2003policies}

Book on senior political activism and the welfare state. Shows how highly participatory groups get their policy preferences fulfilled, and how public policy itself helps create political inequality.

\citem{goerres2010age}

The experience of intergenerational solidarity within the family matters for older people’s attitudes towards public childcare, but it is context-dependent.

\citem{ashok2015support}

Support for redistribution in the U.S. has fallen among the elderly, possibly because they fear universal programs crowding out Medicare.

\citem{vlandas2021ageing}

Literature review on how population ageing affects electoral behaviour, social policy preferences, and public spending.

\citem{munger2022generation}

Book arguing that younger and older generations in the United States are at odds. ``The generation gap is widening into a political fault line.''

\citem{sorelle2023political}

Young voters more likely to support candidates enacting student debt relief.





\section{Drivers of beliefs}

\subsection{Shared history}

\citem{mannheim1952sociological}

Generations are shaped not by simple biological aging, but by shared social and historical locations during formative years.

\citem{alesina2007good}

Time spent under Communism increases demand for redistribution. Tested in East Germany.

\citem{landier2008investigating}

Evidence of slow, home-biased and path-dependent learning about the comparative virtues of economic systems.

\citem{fuchs2015endogeneity}

Time spent under democracy increases support for democracy.

\subsection{Formative years}

\citem{krosnick1989aging}

Using ANES data, the authors find support for the impressionable years hypothesis (highly susceptible to change in late adolescence/early adulthood) over the increasing persistence hypothesis (gradually more resistant to change over lifecycle) for political attitudes.

\citem{malmendier2011depression}

Early ``individual experiences of macroeconomic shocks'' paper. Individuals who experienced low stock market returns throughout their lives reported lower willingness to take financial risk, with stronger effects on young people.

\citem{roth2018experienced}

People who have experienced higher inequality during their lives are less in favor of redistribution. Uses European Social Survey, U.S. GSS, and German GSS.

\citem{giuliano2024aggregate}

Review of theory and empirics on how aggregate shocks impact political preferences, risk attitudes, and trust in institutions. The effect of a shock experienced during young adulthood is stronger and longer lasting.

\subsection{Recessions/shocks}

\citem{margalit2013explaining}

During the GFC, the personal experience of economic hardship, particularly the loss of a job, had a major effect on increasing support for welfare
spending. However, the large attitudinal shift was short lived, dissipating
as individuals’ employment situations improved.

\citem{owens2013material}

Preferences for redistribution are malleable: individuals want more redistribution when they experience unemployment or lose household income.

\citem{carreri2023economic}

Politicians who experienced a recession hold more conservative positions on redistribution.

\citem{giuliano2023recessions}

Individuals who experienced a recession when young are less in favor of redistribution, evidence for impressionable years hypothesis. Uses regional variation in U.S. GSS, validated by Integrated Values Survey (WVS/EVS).

\subsection{Unmet expectations}

\citem{kurer2022disappointed}

Using German household panel data, estimates difference between status expectations during childhood and outcomes realized in adulthood. Voters who fall short are politically dissatisfied, associated with higher abstention rates, less mainstream policy support, and more radical voting.

\citem{cox2024great}

65\% of Chilean students have unmet expectations, which induces a shift toward progovernment/proequality ideology.

\subsection{Competitive environment}

\citem{carvalho2023zero}

Demotivating beliefs (envy of others, belief in the importance of luck, disdain for competitive
effort, and traditional beliefs in witchcraft) evolve when interactions are zero-sum, i.e., one person's gain comes at the expense of others.

Introduces Character-Role Narrative Framework as a tool to systematically analyze narratives. Within the context of a topic, the essence of a narrative is captured by its characters in one of three essential roles: hero, villain, and victim.
