\begin{abstract}
Young Americans support redistribution far more strongly than older Americans, and this age gap has widened substantially over recent decades. Using a purpose-built survey of roughly 2{,}000 adults, balanced equally between those younger and older than 30, we test a broad range of explanations for the gap---economic self-interest, life-cycle and consumption-smoothing motives, exposure to long-run risks, and differences in beliefs, values, and time preferences. A robustness analysis isolates only two systematically robust and quantitatively large determinants: zero-sum beliefs and the belief that progressive taxation blunts incentives. Income and financial position, exposure to risk, and social-mobility beliefs play little role, and demographics account for only a modest share. Differences in zero-sum thinking alone explain the majority of the young--old gap. We replicate these patterns in independent cross-sectional and longitudinal surveys---the GSS, the ANES, and \citet{chinoy2026zero}---and show that the age gradient has steepened markedly since the 1970s, with the widening in zero-sum beliefs accounting for a sizable share of the trend. We conclude by examining what may have driven the change in the age distribution of zero-sum beliefs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In the United States, at any given point in time, young people are much more supportive of government intervention to reduce inequality than older people are. Figure~\ref{fig:age-gradient} presents this pattern in four recent surveys: (i) our own 2025 survey of roughly 2{,}000 adults split equally between those younger and older than 30, (ii) the survey of \citet{chinoy2026zero}, (iii) the most recent wave of the General Social Survey (GSS, 2021), and (iv) the most recent wave of the American National Election Study (ANES, 2024). All four reveal a strongly significant cross-sectional \textit{age gradient}---equivalently, a \textit{generational gap}---in the intensity of support for redistribution. Earlier surveys display the same gradient, though its steepness has evolved over time and is most pronounced in recent years.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{graphs/fig1_age_gradient_recent.pdf}
    \caption{The age gradient in support for redistribution, across four independent datasets. Each panel plots the mean response ($\pm 1$ standard error) by age bracket, using the most recent wave of each source. In every dataset, support is highest among the youngest respondents and declines steadily with age.}
    \label{fig:age-gradient}
\end{figure}


This paper is the first to focus specifically on documenting and understanding this age gradient, using a survey designed expressly to test competing explanations for why young people hold more pro-redistributive attitudes.

One straightforward hypothesis is that young people are on average poorer than older people and would therefore gain more from redistribution (the \emph{income} hypothesis). A related version holds that they view redistribution as a way to smooth income over the life cycle (the \emph{life-cycle} hypothesis). A third is that young people face larger risks going forward---for example, climate and AI risk---and so place greater value on the safety net that redistribution provides (the \emph{long-run-risk} hypothesis). A fourth is that, uncertain of their eventual position in the income distribution, they wish to hedge against the worst outcomes from behind a veil of ignorance (the \emph{Rawlsian} hypothesis).

A second class of hypotheses concerns \emph{beliefs} about the economic game people think they are playing. One possibility, suggested by \citet{chinoy2026zero}, is that young people engage in more zero-sum thinking than older people. A companion idea is that they perceive society as offering fewer opportunities for upward mobility. Another is that they hold different beliefs about the disincentive effects of redistributive taxation, or about the incentive benefits of high incomes. Finally, one can ask whether generational differences in patience, or in the inclination to give (to friends or to strangers), correlate with support for redistribution.

A robustness analysis reveals that zero-sum beliefs and the belief that redistributive taxation is disincentivizing are the only systematically robust and sizable determinants of support for redistribution. Income and financial position, and future income risk, play virtually no role, while other candidates---such as beliefs about social mobility or exposure to particular risks---matter only marginally. Moreover, the young--old difference in zero-sum beliefs explains the majority of the difference in their support for redistribution: other robust correlates, such as the perceived disincentive effect of taxation, display no age gradient of their own. Demographic variables account for only a modest share of the difference in zero-sum thinking. We confirm the main results of our survey using the information available in other surveys.

Using long-running, repeatedly fielded surveys (the GSS and ANES), we further establish that the age gradient between young and old people has widened considerably over time. Figure~\ref{fig:widening} compares the GSS and ANES across waves; the gap has roughly \red{(doubled--tripled)}. Finally, we test several hypotheses about this growing age polarization in attitudes toward redistribution. We show that the widening gap in zero-sum thinking explains a sizable share of the change in the age gradient over time, and we examine what may have driven the underlying change in beliefs.

\subsection{Cohort effects or age effects?}

The age, period, and cohort (APC) effects cannot be separately identified without additional restrictions. A \emph{widening} of the gradient, however, cannot come from period effects alone; it must reflect cohort effects or time-varying age effects. The figures below offer suggestive evidence. Generations start at different mean levels---partly explained by the positive period effects in Table~\ref{table:multi-period-regression}, but potentially also reflecting positive cohort effects. Preferences are fairly stable over time within a generation, suggesting cohort effects, while a mild decline over the life cycle suggests some age effects as well (especially if period effects trend in the opposite direction). Later sections test whether the underlying mechanisms are more consistent with the age/life-cycle hypothesis or the cohort/imprinting hypothesis.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{graphs/pair_apc_GSS_GOVEQINC.pdf}
    \caption{GSS (\textsc{goveqinc}): mean support by age bracket (left) and by survey year (right), plotted separately for each birth generation. Generations begin at different levels and are fairly flat over the life cycle, pointing to a cohort component in the gradient.}

Three main hypotheses: (i) current income and financial strain, which is the level of financial position, (ii) perceived income/career/other financial risk (climate, housing prices, etc.), which is the variance of financial position, and (iii) expected trajectory and consumption smoothing, which is the path of financial position. Current unemployment, financial satisfaction, savings levels, and income belong to (i). The variance and lower bound of expected income and perceived exposure to climate/AI/housing risk belong to (ii). Expected growth or stagnation in income, the upper bound of expected income, myopia and time-discounting belong in (iii). 

The most simple way to test (i)–(iii) would be head-to-head against other variables. We could construct variables that proxy for each channel, including interactions such as myopia x expected income growth or downside income risk x perceived mobility, then compare the R-squared attributed to each channel in a Shapley decomposition. We will probably find that beliefs matter much more. 

So far, we find statistically significant but quantitatively small evidence that redistribution functions as insurance for longer-term risks which young people are more exposed to such as income/career uncertainty, housing, climate, and AI; weak evidence that redistribution functions as relief from present financial stress, which may be higher among young people; weak evidence that young people, who are often poorer earlier in their careers, favor redistribution due to myopia or time-discounting; and weak evidence that young people support redistribution because of misperceived future income needs or lack of experience with taxation.

\subsection{Shapley decomposition: most-important predictors of preferences}

A Shapley decomposition splits a regression's R-squared across predictors. To handle correlated predictors fairly, the method averages each predictor's marginal contribution over all possible orderings of entry. Shares are non-negative and sum to the full model's R-squared, so each number is interpreted as that variable's percentage of explained variance. In the ``by cluster'' tables, we simply sum the percentage explained by each variable in the cluster.

In the tables below, Shapley decompositions for a range of surveys suggest that beliefs about the zero-sumness of wealth and social/racial equality are the most important predictors of redistributive preferences, followed by beliefs about deservingness/attribution, aggregate efficiency, and opportunity/mobility.

\subsection{Gelbach decomposition}

Methodology: Our Gelbach decomposition compares a ``short'' model with only age as a covariate to a ``full'' model with age and many other variables (in this case, beliefs) as covariates. Our full model will feature a smaller coefficient (in absolute value) on the age covariate, suggesting an omitted variable bias in the short model that will be attributed to different belief covariates. If a belief is negatively correlated with age and positively correlated with demand for redistribution (or vice-versa), it will be an important/highly-ranked contributor in the Gelbach decomposition. If it is correlated with age but not preferences, or preferences but not age, it will not show up in the decomposition.

As we would expect, zerosumrich is highly-ranked because it is negatively correlated with age and positively correlated with preferences. However, taxdemotivates and gapmotivates are unimportant in the decomposition (i.e., do not explain the age gradient) because, while they are highly correlated with preferences, they are not correlated with age. For the progressivity outcome, beliefs about zero-sum wealth and zero-sum DEI combine to explain the majority of the age gradient.
