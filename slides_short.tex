\begin{frame}{Life Cycle vs Beliefs?}

Two competing hypotheses: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lifecycle: currently low  income + time discounting, present financial stress, long-term career/lifestyle risk, prospect of upward mobility. 
    
    \vspace{0.2em}
    Largely \textit{self-interest}-driven.
    \item Belief divergence: experienced inequality, growth, mobility, housing prices, social safety net access, diversity, immigration. Shaped by political narratives and the supply of politicians/platforms.
    % (who to blame) (electoral institutions)

    \vspace{0.2em}
    Largely \textit{belief}-driven.
    % Real economic conditions 
\end{itemize}
    
\end{frame}



\begin{frame}{Summary of findings}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item It is not the economic position of the young: 26 measures of income, risk and prospects explain none of the gap.
    \item It is what the young believe about the economy: zero-sum and deservingness beliefs account for much of the gap.
    \item The age gap on redistribution grew from zero in the 1970s–80s to +0.5 SD today (half of the party gap, similar to the race gap, double the gender gap).
    \item A similar age gap in beliefs has emerged. 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Ongoing analysis: what drives these beliefs?
    \end{itemize}
    % the US young-old gap in support for redistribution
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Discussion: is it zero-sum or deservingness?}

Related but distinct beliefs
\begin{itemize}
    \item Positive-sum vs zero-sum: whether one's effort can grow the pie or one's gains are necessarily another's losses. 
    % distribution
    \item Attribution/deservingness: whether one's gains were deserved or earned unfairly. %(reflecting growing inequality).
\end{itemize}
Observations
\begin{itemize}
    \item Young people on average believe that CEOs are zero-sum but not DEI initiatives; zero-sum is not a universal belief.
    \item ``Tax demotivates'' and ``gap motivates'' address positive-sumness and predict preferences but have no age gap. 
\end{itemize}
    
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Comparing to experimental evidence: the experiment}

\small
\textbf{Alm{\aa}s, Cappelen and Tungodden (2020, JPE):} spectators make a \textbf{real} distributive choice for two strangers.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spectators:} 1{,}000 Americans and 1{,}000 Norwegians, largely representative samples (Research Now / Norstat), ages 18--96.
    \item \textbf{Workers:} two MTurk workers do the same task; one is initially paid \$6, the other \$0.
    \item \textbf{Choice:} the spectator redistributes, from 6--0 (no redistribution) to 3--3 (equal split); the workers are paid accordingly.
    \item \textbf{Randomized source of inequality:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Luck}: the \$6 was assigned by lottery;
        \item \textit{Merit}: the \$6 went to the more productive worker;
        \item \textit{Efficiency}: lottery, but redistribution is costly (the pie shrinks).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Outcome:} implemented inequality $= |a-b|/(a+b) \in [0,1]$; 0 = equal split, 1 = no redistribution.
    \item \textbf{Age gradient:} slope of implemented inequality on age within each country $\times$ treatment cell ($\approx 333$ spectators each).
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Discussion: Requiem for Alesina}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Alesina, Glaeser \& Sacerdote (Brookings, 2001); Alesina \& Glaeser, \textit{Fighting Poverty in the US and Europe} (2004)
    \item Standard economic explanations do not fit the US--Europe gap
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Pre-tax inequality, income volatility, social mobility, efficiency costs of taxation
    \end{itemize}
    \item Two explanations remain
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Racial heterogeneity and racial resentment}: redistribution to the poor, who are disproportionately Black, is unappealing to many voters
        \item Political institutions: majoritarian elections, federalism and courts protecting property limited a socialist party and the political power of the poor
    \end{itemize}
    \item Two new findings (ANES)
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Racial resentment has fallen, especially among the young: 3.4 to 2.6 for the young vs.\ 3.5 to 3.0 for the old (1--5 scale, 2008--12 to 2020--24)
        \item The young are more diverse: white share 56\% among the young vs.\ 74\% among the old in 2020--24 (87\% vs.\ 91\% in 1972--76)
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conclusions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The US age gap in support for redistribution is new and still growing.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Close to zero in the 1970s--80s; it steepened after 2000 and jumped in 2020--24 (ANES age gradient: $0.05$ to $0.12$ SD per decade since 2000).
    \end{itemize}
    \item It is not the economic position of the young.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item In our 2025 survey, 26 measures of income, risk and prospects explain none of the gap; zero-sum beliefs about the rich are the largest part of what is explained.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Over four decades, the gap moves with the young's racial and social attitudes, not with their economic circumstances.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The five strongest non-political predictors, led by racial resentment, moral traditionalism and race, account for 63\% of the within-year gap (ANES, 1986--2024).
        \item Their young--old gaps widened after 2000 and track the steepening until 2020; income, class and homeownership gaps did not move.
        \item None of them accounts for the 2022--24 jump.
    \end{itemize}
    \item A generational divide is also visible directly: the US has the highest perceived young--old conflict, and Social Security is the one program the young want less of.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Measuring absolute mobility}
\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Definition} (Chetty et al.\ 2017, ``The Fading American Dream''): share of children who earn more than their parents, comparing real household income at age $\approx 30$.
    \item \textbf{Source}: Online Data Table 2, state $\times$ decadal birth cohort (1940s--1980s).
    \item \textbf{Assignment} (Prolific 2025): each respondent gets the value for the state they \emph{grew up in} and their birth decade ($2025 - \text{age}$).
    \begin{itemize}\footnotesize
        \item It is the realized mobility of one's own cohort in one's home state (an outcome at $\approx 30$), not mobility observed during childhood.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scaling}: z-scored across respondents; $+$ $=$ higher mobility.

\end{itemize}
